Many coaches and consultants start with Instagram. It is quick to set up, familiar to clients, and often enough to secure the first conversations. If people already contact you there, a website does not suddenly make Instagram less useful.

A website starts to earn its cost when potential clients also search on Google, your services are difficult to explain in a few posts, or your podcast and articles are spread across several platforms. It gives those people a clear place to understand your work and contact you.

## What Instagram does well

Instagram helps people discover the person behind a service. A coach can share a short exercise, answer a common doubt, or show how a session works. A consultant can comment on a current problem and start a conversation in the same place. Posting regularly builds familiarity before someone is ready to enquire.

For a new service, that may be all you need. A clear profile, useful posts, and an easy way to send a message can test whether people understand the offer and want it. There is little value in commissioning a substantial website before you know what clients ask for or which service they choose.

Keep using Instagram when it brings relevant enquiries. The decision about a website should begin with the gaps you can see, rather than a rule that every business must have one.

## When a website helps

### Being found on Google

Consider someone searching for _sports psychologist Lisbon_ or _how to handle competition nerves_. They already know what problem they want to solve, but they may have no coach in mind. A dedicated page about the service gives Google something useful to show them; an article can answer the second search and lead them towards the relevant service.

Instagram posts rarely appear prominently in searches like these. A website therefore opens a different route to your business. You can create one page for each service, use the words clients actually search for, and answer their practical questions about format, audience, and contact.

You do not pay for every visit from these searches. Useful pages can also [continue attracting readers long after publication](https://digitalxperience.pt/site-profissional-vs-redes-sociais/), provided that they remain accurate and answer a real question.

### Explaining your services

A feed presents information in the order it was published. New visitors may need to open several posts, Stories, and profile links before they understand what you offer. Important details also compete with announcements and day-to-day content.

On a website, you choose the path. A visitor can see your services, who they are for, how you work, and how to enquire without reconstructing the offer from recent posts. This matters for coaching and consulting because clients often compare several providers before making contact.

The contact step can be equally simple. A short form can collect a name, email address, and the reason for the enquiry. You receive enough context to reply usefully, while the visitor avoids explaining everything in a sequence of direct messages.

### Gathering your work

Podcasts, articles, interviews, and downloadable guides often end up on separate services. A website can bring them together under your name. Someone who hears you on a podcast can find the related article; a reader can move from that article to the service it supports.

You also decide how this material is presented and organised. Instagram still distributes new content to followers, while the website keeps an accessible collection that is easier to browse and search. If an account is restricted, hacked, or closed, your domain and the material published there remain another route through which people can find and contact you.

A website can strengthen credibility as well. Around 84% of consumers consider a business with its own website more credible than one present only on social media, according to [Verisign data cited by Webfy](https://www.webfy.pt/pt/blog/site-proprio-vs-redes-sociais-o-que-traz-mais-clientes). That figure does not mean any website creates trust. Clear information, current content, and visible contact details still determine what a visitor sees.

## Filipa Marques's website

Filipa Marques is a sports psychology and NLP coach who helps athletes perform under pressure. Before the website, she already had a podcast, published regular content, and had built an engaged following around her work. Instagram had proved that people were interested in her work.

The missing piece was a single place to understand the full offer. Her podcast lived alongside content published elsewhere, and a person searching for mental-performance support had no clear set of pages explaining her services.

I built her website as part of a complete rebrand. Each service received its own page, written and structured so that Google could find it and potential clients could understand it. The website also brought her method, podcast, and articles together, with a direct route to contact her.

This changed the role of her existing material. An Instagram follower could visit the website for more detail before enquiring. A person with no previous connection to Filipa could discover an article in Google, understand her approach, and then explore the relevant service.

## When Instagram is enough

Stay with a simple Instagram setup while you are testing a new offer, especially if clients already understand it and direct messages are manageable. A low-cost product bought quickly from a link in the profile may gain little from a custom website at that stage.

A website becomes more useful when repeated questions are taking up your messages, clients search for the service on Google, or your useful content has become hard to navigate. It may also help when enquiries need more context than a direct message usually provides.

Do not use follower count as the deciding factor. A consultant with a small, specialised audience may need clear service pages because each enquiry involves careful comparison. A creator with a much larger audience may still work effectively with Instagram and one purchase link.

## A practical check

Look at recent client conversations and searches, then ask:

- **How did people find you?** If some searched for your name, profession, or location on Google, check what they currently see.
- **Can a new visitor understand each service quickly?** List the questions you repeatedly answer in messages. Those answers may belong on a service page.
- **Is your useful material easy to revisit?** Count how many places someone must search to find your podcast, articles, and key resources.
- **Can people contact you in the right way?** Decide whether a direct message gives you enough information to reply and arrange the next step.

One recurring gap can justify a small website. You do not need to wait until every point applies, and the first version does not need dozens of pages.

## Frequently asked questions

**Can I just use Instagram instead of a website?**

Yes. Instagram can be enough while you test an offer, handle a manageable number of enquiries, and do not depend on Google searches. A website becomes useful when you need clear service pages, an organised collection of content, or a better contact route.

**Does a website help you rank on Google better than Instagram?**

Usually. Google can show a dedicated service page or article for the exact question someone searches. Instagram profiles and posts may appear, but they give you much less control over which information Google can present.

**How much does a website cost for a coach or small business in Portugal?**

A custom website typically costs roughly €800 to €3,000, depending on its size and the services it needs to connect to ([Obvious Bubbles pricing overview](https://obviousbubbles.pt/pt/blog/site-para-pequenas-empresas-portugal/)). A small information site sits near the lower end; a rebrand with a podcast, booking, and a larger content library costs more.

**Should I stop posting on Instagram once I have a website?**

No. Keep Instagram if it helps people discover and trust your work. Use the website for detailed service information, material people may want to revisit, Google searches, and structured enquiries.
