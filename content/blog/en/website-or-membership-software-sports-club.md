After a tournament, somebody at the club needs to publish the results, photos, and details of the next event. On another day, the treasurer needs to identify outstanding fees, confirm a registration, and prepare figures for the accountant. Each group of tasks needs its own tool.

That distinction shaped the website I built for CDEFF — Clube Desportivo Escola Francisco Franco, a sports club in Madeira. The website handles public information. Registrations, member records, payments, and accounting exports belong in member management software. Separating the work helps a club compare quotes and direct its budget towards the bigger operational problem.

## What each tool handles

A club website serves players, families, sponsors, and people discovering the organisation for the first time. It gives them a stable place to find news, fixtures, contact details, tournament information, and results. Joining instructions should point to the registration form.

Member management software serves the board, administrative volunteers, and treasurer. It records members and players, tracks annual or monthly fees, handles or confirms payments, and exports information. When the annual meeting approaches or the accountant requests a file, the current records should come from this system.

A modern website can sit alongside a spreadsheet full of unidentified bank transfers. Equally, a club can run its membership efficiently while posting every tournament update on social media alone. List each failing task and its owner.

## The CDEFF example

The [CDEFF website](https://www.cdeff.com/) publishes club activities, articles, and tournament details. Visitors can understand what the club does and find current information without searching through months of social posts.

The club updates this information in Notion. Put simply, Notion is the tool where a volunteer writes or changes a news item, result, or tournament entry. The website then displays that content to visitors. Updating it feels much like editing an ordinary document, so the person handling communications needs no programming knowledge.

This also makes handovers between volunteers easier. Whoever takes over next season can find the existing content and update it through the same familiar process. Keeping the news and calendar current should take around two hours per month **[estimate — confirm]**, depending on how many activities the club publishes.

CDEFF's website does not keep membership records or collect fees. Players have no private account for making payments, renewing their registration, or receiving a membership card. We set that boundary at the start, allowing the public website to stay focused and avoiding an administrative system that the club would then have to operate and maintain.

## When management comes first

Prioritise management software if the treasurer spends evenings matching bank transfers to names in a spreadsheet. The same applies when volunteers copy registration details between files, lose track of application status, or take hours to prepare accounting exports.

Published prices from Portuguese providers give clubs a useful spending range:

- **Adjunto** offers club member management (_gestão de sócios_) with a members module included, priced around 45€/month or 450€/year, per its [pricing page](https://www.adjunto.pt/pt/gestao-socios).
- **Em Jogo** sells member management as a paid extra in the 100–150€/year range, and bundles club management plus a website for roughly 390€/year, according to its [plans and pricing](https://emjogo.pt/planos-e-precos-software-gestao-clubes-desportivos/).
- **SM Sócios** positions member management software between 135€ and 385€/year, with Multibanco references for payments and SAFT export for accounting, as its [site](https://www.smsocios.pt/) describes.

At these prices, commissioning a custom system for dues and payments is usually hard to justify. An established product may already provide Multibanco references, payment history, and SAFT exports. Those features take time to recreate and test.

Personal data adds a practical concern. A management tool may hold names, contact details, and dates of birth for junior players. The club must control access, correct records, keep backups, and restore information after a failure. Those responsibilities deserve careful assessment before replacing an established service with custom software.

## When the website comes first

Build the website first when administration remains manageable but public information is scattered or outdated. A typical club might announce each event in a different social post, answer the same questions through private messages, and have no clear page to send a potential sponsor.

Start with a small set of useful content: news, fixtures, tournaments, contact details, and joining instructions. Assign responsibility for each update as well. Without an owner, even a well-designed website soon displays old dates and incomplete results.

Social media still helps the club reach people, but it performs poorly as a permanent record. New posts push tournament rules and match reports down the feed. Parents struggle to find an older result, while sponsors receive a collection of links instead of one clear overview. The website keeps long-lived information organised and accessible.

## How to set the priority

Observe one normal week at the club and note the recurring tasks. The pattern usually identifies the better first investment.

- **Choose the website first** when people repeatedly ask about the next tournament, recent results, or joining the club, while a short membership list remains easy to manage.
- **Choose management software first** when payments go unidentified, registrations live in several files, reminders are prepared manually, or accounting exports take hours.
- **Plan both projects** when administration has exceeded the spreadsheet and public communication also needs work. Request separate prices, check what each includes, and implement the tool that removes the largest current delay.

Test products with actual club work. In a software demonstration, register a player, record a fee, find an overdue payment, and create the export requested by the accountant. For a website, time how long it takes to publish a news item, change a tournament date, and hand the task to another volunteer. Feature lists show what a product claims to offer; these exercises confirm whether the club can use it comfortably.

## Frequently asked questions

### Does a sports club need a website or member management software?

Most established clubs benefit from both. The website publishes news, fixtures, tournaments, and contact details; management software records registrations, fees, and payments. If the budget covers one tool now, choose the one that removes the most time-consuming work.

### How much does club member management software cost in Portugal?

For off-the-shelf tools aimed at Portuguese clubs, expect roughly a few hundred euros a year. Adjunto lists around 450€/year, Em Jogo sells member management from about 100–150€/year (or a club-plus-website bundle near 390€/year), and SM Sócios ranges from 135€ to 385€/year. All are far cheaper than a custom-built dues system.

### Can one system handle both the website and member management?

Some providers bundle a basic website with their management software. This can suit a club that needs simple pages and prefers a single service. Check whether volunteers can easily publish news and tournaments, then verify that the administrative side covers registrations, payments, and the exports required by the accountant.
