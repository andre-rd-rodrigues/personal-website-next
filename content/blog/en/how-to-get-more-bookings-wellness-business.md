Good treatments cannot lead to bookings if a client opens the website and cannot find a way to make an appointment. Missing reviews create another pause, while a slow page on a phone may end the visit before the client has even seen the treatment list.

Brennda Castro is a massage therapist in Quinta do Conde with more than five years of experience, and her website needed to show the treatments and studio with the same care she gives her clients. The next step also had to be obvious: choose a treatment and request a booking.

## Where bookings are lost

Picture someone visiting after a recommendation. They want to see which massages are available, understand what the studio feels like, and check whether previous clients had a good experience. If the answers are reassuring, they look for a way to book.

That simple path often becomes harder than it should be. The phone number may sit in the footer, the booking option may be inside a menu, or the visitor may have to leave the treatment page to find reviews. Each extra search gives them another opportunity to close the page and continue elsewhere.

## Keep booking within reach

On Brennda's site, the booking button remains visible in the navigation. It appears again after the information a client uses to choose. Someone who already knows what they want can act immediately, while a client who needs time to read finds the same option when ready.

Online booking can influence which provider a person chooses. [GetApp research cited by Koalendar](https://koalendar.com/pt-br/blog/como-conseguir-mais-agendamentos-online) found that 94% of consumers are more likely to choose a service provider that offers it. Hiding the option inside a menu adds work after the client has already decided to take the next step.

The issue also appears outside wellness businesses. I included it in an article on [five design mistakes that can harm a business](/blog/5-critical-ux-ui-mistakes-that-can-harm-your-business). Here, the practical fix is straightforward: keep the button visible and label it so the client knows what will happen after tapping it.

## Place testimonials near treatments

A new massage or aesthetics client may have practical concerns about an unfamiliar studio. Is it welcoming, and will the therapist listen carefully? Testimonials let previous clients answer those questions in their own words and describe whether the experience matched the photographs.

On Brennda's site, we kept real testimonials close to the treatment descriptions and booking button. A visitor can learn what a massage involves and read about another client's experience without changing pages, while a separate reviews page would rely on them deciding to look for it.

Specific testimonials help. Comments about the welcome, the therapist's care, or how the person felt after the session give a new client something concrete to consider. Always ask for the client's permission before publishing them.

## Check mobile speed

People often search on a phone. They may be between other tasks or travelling with an unstable mobile connection. Large photographs can then make the page appear frozen before the visitor can read about a treatment or reach the booking button.

[Landingi's health and wellness guidance, based on Unbounce data](https://landingi.com/pt-br/landing-page/melhores-praticas-de-saude/), reports an average conversion rate of about 7.4% for this type of page and recommends one clear primary action alongside fast mobile loading. That percentage is a broad benchmark; each business still needs to measure its own site.

For Brennda, I used a lightweight structure so the treatments, studio, and booking option appear quickly, including on a mid-range phone. You can make a basic check by opening your site without Wi-Fi and timing how long it takes until you can read the page and tap the booking button. My article on [the cost of a slow website](/blog/slow-website-costing-you-money) covers further ways to measure it.

## Follow the booking path

The scheduling service changes what a client experiences. Some calendars open within the website. Others send the client to an external page with different colours, labels, and navigation. Either approach can work, but the whole path should be tested on a phone.

For Brennda's project, we kept the first step lightweight and within her website. Clients can review a treatment and begin their request before dealing with a larger calendar or an unfamiliar screen. The calendar appears later.

Before publishing a wellness website, follow these steps as a new client:

1. Open the home page on a phone.
2. Find a suitable treatment.
3. Read a client testimonial.
4. Tap the booking button.
5. Check what information is requested and when a reply will arrive.

If two actions have equal emphasis, such as calling and completing a form, explain the difference. Buttons labelled **Book online** and **Ask a question** are clearer than two general instructions.

## What to check

Start with one page and an ordinary phone. Check whether the treatment name, duration, price or way to obtain it, and booking button are easy to find. Then ask someone unfamiliar with the website to request an appointment without your help.

Watch where that person hesitates. They may search for reviews, wonder whether the request is confirmed, or tap a button that opens a slow page. This short test gives you clearer evidence than changing colours or photographs at random.

## Frequently asked questions

### How do I get more bookings for a wellness business?

Show the booking button without making people open a menu, place real testimonials near the treatments, and test page speed on a phone. Follow every step through submitting a request to find unclear instructions.

### Where should the booking button go on a wellness website?

Place it in the navigation so it is always available, then repeat it after a treatment description or testimonial. Someone ready to book should find the button without returning to the top of the page.

### Does online booking actually increase appointments?

It can make choosing a time easier and reduce messages exchanged only to find availability. The GetApp research cited above says 94% of consumers are more likely to choose a provider offering online booking, although each business's result depends on its clients and process.
